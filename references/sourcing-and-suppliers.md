# Sourcing & Suppliers

Covers: Strategic Sourcing & Supplier Portfolio Strategy · Supplier Risk & Dependency Management · Supplier Negotiation & Collaborative Recovery

## 1. Strategic sourcing & supplier portfolio strategy

When choosing how to distribute supply across suppliers — whether building a recovery plan or just answering "who should we buy this from" — weigh all of these, not price alone:

- **Unit price** and total cost (including MOQ effects — a cheap unit price with a high minimum order can force overbuying).
- **Lead time** against the actual deadline, not against "fast is always better."
- **Reliability score** and track record — a slightly pricier supplier who reliably delivers is often cheaper in expected-value terms than a discount supplier with a history of slipping.
- **Quality score / certifications**, weighed against `decision-frameworks.md`'s quality governance rules — a hard requirement is a filter, not a scoring input.
- **Geography/concentration** — does this choice increase or reduce dependence on a single region or logistics route?
- **Strategic importance** — is this a component/relationship worth investing in for the long term, or a one-off spot buy?

**Splitting orders** across two or more suppliers is often the right call under disruption, even though it's more complex than picking one vendor: it reduces the chance that a single supplier's failure recreates the same crisis, and it can meet a deadline that no single supplier could meet alone (e.g., 600 units from one, 300 from another). Consider it whenever a single supplier can't fully cover the need, or when concentration risk is already a known concern.

## 2. Supplier risk & dependency management

Before relying on a supplier — especially under time pressure — assess:

- **Concentration risk**: how much of this component (or this business relationship generally) depends on one supplier? A disruption is a good moment to note this even if fixing it isn't in scope right now.
- **Reliability signal**: track their pattern across this engagement. A supplier who gives vague answers, walks back earlier claims, or has a low reliability score should get a lower trust weighting in your decision — and their claims should be verified rather than accepted (see the tracking/verification guidance in `risk-and-continuity.md`, section 5).
- **Financial/operational vulnerability**: if there's a signal the supplier itself is struggling (repeated delays, contradictory communications, capacity claims that don't add up), factor that into how much of the business you're willing to place with them right now, independent of their quoted price.
- **Consequence of over-dependency**: a supplier who is currently reliable but represents 100% of a critical component's supply is still a standing risk — flag it, even when the immediate disruption doesn't require action on it.

## 3. Supplier negotiation & collaborative recovery

When recovering supply through a supplier (original or alternate), the goal is a real commitment, not just a hopeful reply. Effective moves:

- **Ask specific, falsifiable questions** rather than open-ended ones. "Can you confirm dispatch and provide a tracking number by end of day" beats "any update?" — it produces something you can verify.
- **Request the concrete terms you need to decide**: exact quantity available, firm delivery date (not a range, if avoidable), price, and whether expediting is possible and at what cost.
- **Consider partial fulfillment** — a supplier who can't meet the full quantity or full timeline immediately may still meaningfully reduce the business impact with a partial shipment now plus the remainder later.
- **Challenge contradictions directly** rather than accepting them. If a supplier's claim conflicts with tracking data or an earlier statement, say so and ask them to reconcile it — don't quietly route around them without giving them the chance to explain, unless time pressure doesn't allow it.
- **Revised commercial terms are a legitimate lever**: expedite fees, revised pricing, or adjusted payment terms can sometimes unlock a recovery that a purely "ask nicely" approach won't. Weigh the cost of these against the cost-of-inaction comparison in `decision-frameworks.md`.

Treat every supplier interaction as evidence for the reliability assessment above — a good response under pressure is a point in their favor for future decisions; a vague or contradicted one is a point against.
