# Financial & Working-Capital Considerations

Covers: Financial & Working-Capital Considerations

## Look past the sticker price

Every emergency action has a financial shadow beyond its headline cost. Before finalizing a recommendation, check for these second-order effects:

- **Emergency procurement premium**: the gap between normal and expedited/emergency pricing is real cost, not just an inconvenience — quantify it and include it in the cost-of-action comparison (`decision-frameworks.md`).
- **Excess or unbalanced stock**: over-ordering "to be safe" ties up working capital and creates its own carrying cost and obsolescence risk, especially for components with volatile demand. A recovery plan that solves today's shortage by creating tomorrow's overstock isn't free.
- **Working capital timing**: emergency purchases often come with less favorable payment terms (faster payment, less negotiating leverage) — this affects cash flow even when the unit economics look acceptable on paper.
- **Cost of downtime**: production downtime isn't just "lost output" — it's lost margin, potential penalty exposure, and possible knock-on cost to fulfil the same customer commitment a different, more expensive way later. Compare this properly against the cost of the intervention rather than anchoring only on the intervention's price tag looking large in isolation.
- **Cost of splitting orders**: splitting across suppliers can raise per-unit admin/logistics overhead slightly — usually worth it for the risk reduction, but worth noting rather than ignoring if the split is large.

## How to use this in a decision

When comparing options in `decision-frameworks.md`'s trade-off table, make sure the "cost" column reflects total financial impact — premium pricing, working-capital effect, and downtime avoided — not just the quoted unit price. A recommendation that only cites unit price invites the wrong comparison and undermines the credibility of the decision brief if a finance-literate approver checks the math.
